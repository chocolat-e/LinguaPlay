gPBL 2026 · Punch English · Evaluation report

How well does the AI coach plan the next round?

Thirty-two simulated learner sessions put through the live between-level curriculum planner, measured against the deterministic fallback planner on identical inputs and checked item-by-item by an independent solver from a different provider family.

1Summary

2System under test

What actually runs when a round ends.

Punch English is a four-lane rhythm-boxing game for learning English. The live round is driven by a deterministic adaptive algorithm; the language model is used only between rounds. When a round ends the game posts a learner report to a local endpoint (/api/punchkt/plan) and asks for one thing back: a plan for the next round — end-of-round feedback, a curriculum, and a pool of exactly 30 multiple-choice questions.

The learner report carries three kinds of evidence, and the prompt requires all three to be used: per-concept mastery and uncertainty; the specific wrong options a player keeps choosing; and which concepts are due for review under a forgetting curve. It also separates motor evidence from language evidence — a punch that never lands is not an English mistake.

The response is constrained by a JSON schema and then checked twice on our side: the schema parse enforces the shape and the 30-question count, and findPlanIssues enforces the semantics — unique question stems, no duplicate options, a misconception label on every wrong option and the literal "none" on the correct one, and a difficulty mix that both totals 30 and matches the questions actually written. A plan that fails either check is sent back once with the list of faults; if the retry also fails, the game silently falls back to a deterministic local planner. That fallback is the baseline this report measures against.

3Method

Where the inputs come from and what counts as evidence.

3.1 Simulated learners

Real play data does not exist at the volume an evaluation needs, so the harness synthesises it. Eight learner profiles are defined by their measurable behaviour — a cold start, a struggling beginner, a player with specific grammar gaps, an advanced player, one whose punches keep missing, one who punches early, one returning after a long gap with decayed retention, and one repeating the same misconception. Each profile is simulated four times with different seeds, giving 32 independent sessions. A single fixed clock is used for the whole batch so every simulated report is reproducible.

The profiles are checked before any billed call: a free phase prints each one's realised accuracy, miss rate and early-punch rate against its target, so a broken generator is caught before it can be mistaken for a broken model.

3.2 The comparison

Every metric below is computed twice — once on the model's plan, once on the deterministic fallback planner's plan for the same learner report — by the same code. That pairing is the point: it answers whether the model earns its cost and latency, rather than whether its output looks plausible in isolation.

3.3 Independent item check

Question quality cannot be graded by the model that wrote the questions. A separate solver from a different provider family answers every generated item without being shown the answer key, and reports its own concern about each item (ambiguous stem, more than one defensible option, no correct option, overlapping options). Agreement between that solver and the plan's key is evidence about the item.

The same solver also answers the 30 human-authored questions already shipped in the game. Those are the control: whatever the solver gets "wrong" on hand-written items is the solver's own error rate, and it sets the floor the generated items should be read against. Disagreements are candidates for review, not verdicts — the harness writes them to disagreements.json for adjudication by hand.

4What is measured

Six groups. Everything except the solver check is computed without a model and without a human, so it can be re-run on any future prompt or model change and compared directly.

Reliability
Whether a usable plan comes back at all: schema-valid rate, acceptance on the first attempt, recovery via the repair retry, and the served rate (no fallback). Reported with Wilson intervals, which stay sensible at rates near 0 and 1. Latency, token usage and billed cost sit here too.
Item soundness
Independent-solver agreement with the answer key, split by difficulty, read against the human-authored control, plus the solver's own concern categories.
Curriculum targeting
Whether the plan addresses what the learner actually needs: precision of the chosen target concepts against the union of weak, uncertain and due concepts (exact and fuzzy name matching, since concept names travel as free text); recall of weak and due concepts; misconception coverage; and the share of questions that sit on the stated curriculum.
Item-writing quality
The biases that make a question bank easy to game: answer-position balance (χ² against uniform), the correct answer being the longest or shortest option, duplicate and near-duplicate stems, and whether distractor labels actually diagnose anything rather than restating "wrong".
Player-facing language
Everything the learner reads is checked for reading grade (Flesch–Kincaid; the prompt targets a twelve-year-old) and for leakage of internal or learning-science vocabulary the prompt bans — "mastery", "misconception", "adaptive", "LLM" and the rest.
Novelty
Whether each session gets fresh material: the share of stems not already in the authored bank, and pairwise stem overlap between plans, which is what catches a model quietly reusing the same thirty questions every round.

6Engineering findings

Defects the evaluation surfaced before it could produce a single number. All four were fixed; the first two also affected the shipped game, not just the harness.

  1. The token-limit parameter is not portable across provider families

    OpenRouter publishes a per-endpoint parameter list, and the request sets require_parameters: true, which discards any endpoint missing a parameter the request sends. The openai/ namespace advertises max_completion_tokens; Google's endpoints advertise max_tokens. Because the code hardcoded max_completion_tokens, every request after the model switch was routed to zero endpoints and returned 404 No endpoints found that can handle the requested parameters.

    This was not confined to the evaluation: the game's own middleware sent the same parameter, so the in-game coach would have failed on every round and silently served the local fallback. Measured directly against the live API, the two families reject the opposite parameter, so a hardcoded name cannot be correct for both — the fix selects the parameter from the model slug.

  2. A 30-item array overflows Gemini's structured-output schema limit

    With routing fixed, the provider began rejecting the request body itself with a bare INVALID_ARGUMENT. Bisecting the schema showed the cause is not a keyword but a size: Gemini expands a minItems/maxItems array into that many copies of the item schema, and 30 copies of an eleven-field question object exceeds its limit. Measured on the same model, eight items pass and sixteen fail, while a hundred copies of a one-field object pass — it is the expanded size that matters, not the count.

    The fix drops length bounds only on large arrays of objects in the schema sent to the provider. Nothing is given up: the count is still stated in the prompt and still enforced on the response by the schema parse and by the difficulty-mix check, which is where a miscount was always caught.

  3. Provider errors were unreadable

    The harness recorded only "Provider returned error" for every provider-side rejection, which is undiagnosable — a bad model slug, an unroutable provider filter and a rejected schema all looked identical. OpenRouter puts the upstream complaint in the error's metadata.raw; the harness now records it, which is how the schema limit above was found.

  4. The solver defaulted to the generator's own provider family

    The independent check defaults its solver to a Google model. Once the generator became a Google model, that default would have had the family grade its own output, quietly converting the strongest evidence in the report into a measure of self-agreement. The solver is pinned to a different family for this run.

7Threats to validity

  • Simulated learners, not real ones. The reports are generated from behavioural profiles. They exercise the planner's input space, but they cannot show whether a plan actually helps a person learn — only whether it is well-formed, well-targeted against the evidence it was given, and sound as English.
  • The targeting metrics flatter the baseline. The deterministic planner selects its target concepts directly from the weak and due lists in the report, so its precision and recall are 1.0 by construction rather than by judgement. The informative comparisons are the ones it cannot win by definition: item novelty, misconception quality, language, and whether questions sit on the curriculum.
  • One solver, one pass. Agreement is evidence about an item, not proof. The authored-question control bounds the solver's own error rate, but disagreements still need adjudication by hand before they are called generator faults.
  • Concept names are matched as free text. Targeting is scored with both exact and fuzzy matching because concept names travel from report to plan as strings; the fuzzy figure is the fairer one and the strict figure is reported beside it.
  • Transport resilience differs from the game. A batch is 32 paid runs in one process, so the harness retries connection failures more times than the game does. This changes no generated token — it only stops a local network drop from voiding a paid batch.

8Reproducing this

Each phase writes its own JSON into eval/results/; the report is rendered from those files, so it cannot drift from the measurements it describes.
